## Page 01 — Traffic Guidance Schemes (TGS) & Traffic Control Plans (TCP)
**URL:** /tgs-traffic-control-plans
The certified diagram that decides whether your work site is safe — and whether it’s approved.
Sightline Traffic Engineers designs traffic control schemes that minimise disruption while maximising safety for every road user, developed in strict
accordance with the Traffic Control at Worksites (TCAWS) Manual and relevant Australian Standards — backed by hands-on experience across civil
and construction projects throughout NSW.
Our process
Consultation — an in-depth discussion to understand your project’s scope, site conditions, and any specific safety concerns
Creation of clear, detailed plans — a certified scheme detailing every required sign, device, marking, cone and speed limit variation
Implementation support — staying engaged through execution, addressing site learnings as they arise and keeping you up to speed throughout
The result: a work area where every road user — workers and pedestrians included — is directed and managed safely through, past, or around the
site. No second-guessing, just a clear, expertly drafted scheme you can rely on.
Closing line, links to the practice page: Your TGS is prepared by the same engineers who design the barrier cross sections and staging sets behind it.

### FAQs
What’s the difference between a TGS and a TCP?
Nothing functionally — Traffic Guidance Scheme (TGS) and Traffic Control Plan (TCP) are used interchangeably across NSW to describe the same certified
diagram of signage, devices, markings and control measures for a work site.
Who can prepare a TGS/TCP?
A suitably qualified and accredited traffic control person or engineer, in accordance with the TCAWS Manual and relevant Australian Standards.
How long does a TGS/TCP take to prepare?
Depends on site complexity — a straightforward single-lane closure turns around quickly; multi-stage or high-traffic sites need more detailed consultation
and design time.
Do I need a new TGS/TCP if site conditions change?
Yes — any material change to the work site, traffic conditions, or staging generally requires the scheme to be reviewed and, if needed, formally updated.

## Page 03 — Traffic Management Plans (TMP & CTMP)
**URL:** /traffic-management-plans
Working without one carries real risk — fines, stop-work orders, and liability if something goes wrong.
Sightline prepares Construction Traffic Management Plans (CTMP) and Traffic Management Plans (TMP) for projects across Sydney and NSW. A TMP
is the strategic document setting out how vehicles, cyclists and pedestrians will be safely managed around a work site, event, or any activity that
disrupts normal road conditions — mandatory for most civil works, developments and events affecting a public road, footpath or cycleway.
What a properly prepared TMP does
Protects vulnerable road users — pedestrians, cyclists, and mobility-impaired road users — with safe alternative routes
Keeps workers safe from live traffic and site vehicle movements
Maintains reasonable traffic flow for the public, minimising delay and disruption
Meets all relevant state and local requirements, including TfNSW codes of practice
When a TMP is required
Generally wherever a project will alter normal road, footpath or cycleway conditions for any duration; form part of a DA condition; require a Road
Occupancy Licence or similar permit; or have a measurable impact on the surrounding road network due to its scale or duration.
What’s inside a TMP
Site & Hazard Assessment — traffic volumes and patterns, nearby schools, hospitals and transport stops, sightlines and road geometry, and a
risk matrix mapping hazards to controls
Traffic Guidance Scheme (TGS) — the visual component, prepared in line with the Austroads Guide to Temporary Traffic Management
Implementation & Monitoring Plan — roles and responsibilities, set-up, modification and removal procedures, contingency plans, and ongoing
inspections
TMP vs TGS vs VMP
TMP — the master strategy: full risk assessment, procedures, responsibilities and compliance framework
TGS (also called a Traffic Control Plan) — the visual blueprint traffic controllers use on the ground
VMP (Vehicle Movement Plan) — manages vehicle logistics inside the site boundary, distinct from the TGS’s public road focus
Our process
1. Initial consultation & site assessment — scoping works and assessing road geometry, sightlines and existing activity
2. Risk assessment & strategy development — identifying hazards and developing controls in line with safety-in-design principles
3. TGS/VMP design — standards-compliant diagrams (AS 1742.3) showing signage, barriers, VMS and personnel
4. Documentation & submission — compiling and lodging a single compliant plan
5. Implementation & monitoring — pre-start briefings, on-site support, and ongoing review
Common mistakes we help clients avoid
Relying on a generic, non-site-specific template — every location has its own hazards a template can’t account for
Overlooking pedestrian and cyclist provisions — a compliant TMP needs safe, accessible alternative routes
Treating a technically sound plan as “job done” — a TMP is only as good as its on-site implementation

### FAQs
Who is qualified to design a TMP in NSW?
A Transport for NSW “Prepare a Work Zone Traffic Management Plan” qualification (equivalent accreditation applies in other states). Always engage an
appropriately certified consultant.
What’s the difference between a TMP and a TIA?
A TMP is an operational document for managing traffic around a temporary work site or event. A TIA is a strategic report for a DA, assessing a
development’s longer-term impact on the surrounding road network.
Can an approved TMP be changed once work starts?
Not without a formal amendment — the plan must be revised by a qualified designer and resubmitted for approval.
What standards apply?
AS 1742.3:2019 for signs, devices and procedures, alongside the Austroads Guide to Temporary Traffic Management and relevant state codes, including the
TCAWS Manual in NSW.


## Page 04 — Complex & Multi-Stage Traffic Management Plans
**URL:** /complex-multi-stage-tmp
Getting the handover between stages wrong is one of the most common sources of delay and safety risk on large projects.
Large infrastructure projects rarely operate under a single, static traffic arrangement. Bridge upgrades, road widenings, major intersection works,
and civil infrastructure projects typically progress through multiple construction phases, each with its own access requirements, barrier
arrangements, and network impact. Sightline prepares the multi-stage TMPs that bring these elements together into one coherent delivery
framework.
A standard TMP is built around one traffic arrangement for one work site. A complex TMP is built around a sequence — Stage 1, Stage 2, Stage 3 and
beyond — where the traffic arrangement, barrier positions, pedestrian routes, and construction vehicle access all change as the project progresses.
Scope
Staged CAD traffic layouts — showing construction status at every stage
Barrier design & cross sections — confirmed at each stage, not just at project completion
Vehicle swept path confirmation — reconfirmed at each stage rather than assumed to carry over
Traffic modelling / SIDRA analysis — where the project affects intersection performance, at each stage
Staging transition documentation — clear drawing conventions showing what changes between stages
Risk assessment — reassessed across the full project timeline, not just once at the start
Our process
1. Scope the full staging sequence up front, covering the entire project lifecycle
2. Design staging layouts, barrier cross sections, and swept path plans together for each stage
3. Model network impact where relevant, confirming each stage — not only the final layout
4. Document transitions clearly for unambiguous handover on site
5. Submit the staged set to the relevant authority, tracking revisions stage by stage
6. Support delivery through construction, confirming design intent and managing amendments

### FAQs
How does this differ from a standard TMP?
A standard TMP covers one arrangement for one site. A complex TMP covers a full sequence of stages, with staging, barrier, and swept path design
developed for each phase — not just the final layout.
Does every stage need its own swept path and barrier design?
Where vehicle access, lane configuration, or barrier positions change between stages — yes. Carrying an earlier stage’s design forward unchecked is a
common and avoidable compliance gap.
Are mid-project amendments supported?
Yes — revised and reissued through the standard design change process, with revision history tracked on each sheet.
What’s needed to scope a complex TMP?
The full project programme covering all planned stages, existing site geometry and survey, and the construction vehicles expected at each phase.


## Page 05 — Temporary Barrier Design Statements & Cross Sections
**URL:** /temporary-barrier-design
An incorrect deflection zone is a common cause of rejection — or barrier failure in the field.
Where a project requires temporary safety barriers — concrete, steel, or water-filled — Sightline prepares the cross-section drawings and design
statements confirming the barrier system, deflection zones, and construction zone widths meet road authority requirements.
Cross-section drawings show the existing surface, staging design surface, and ultimate design surface side by side, with lane widths, shoulder widths,
deflection zones, and the approved barrier system specified at each chainage point along the works.

### FAQs
What is a deflection zone?
The clear space behind a temporary barrier required for the barrier to move into if struck, as specified by the manufacturer. Getting this wrong is one of the
most common reasons a barrier design gets rejected — or fails in the field.
Are all barrier types covered?
Yes — concrete, steel, and water-filled systems, each checked against the road authority’s approved barrier list and the manufacturer’s specification,
whichever is more stringent.
What information is needed to produce a cross section?
Existing survey and road geometry, the intended construction zone width, and a proposed barrier type if one is already identified. We can also recommend
a barrier system based on site conditions.
Can cross sections change during construction?
Yes — revised and reissued through the standard design change process as staging or construction conditions evolve.


## Page 06 *(source note: the only page that explains swept path analysis — other pages link here)* — Swept Path Analysis (Vehicle Tracking)
**URL:** /swept-path-analysis
A design that looks fine on paper can still be too narrow for the vehicle it’s built for.
Swept path analysis evaluates the space a vehicle needs to complete a turning manoeuvre. Because a vehicle’s rear wheels track a tighter arc than
its front wheels, this difference has to be accounted for to keep roads, driveways, junctions, and parking areas safe and functional. Get it wrong, and
the result is costly redesigns, safety hazards, or vehicles encroaching into pedestrian and cyclist space.
Where it matters
Residential developments — balancing everyday car access with the turning needs of larger service vehicles like garbage trucks and buses,
without over-engineering road widths in a way that encourages speeding
Retail and industrial sites — testing loading bay and yard layouts against large or articulated delivery vehicles before construction locks the
design in
Schools — designing drop-off and pick-up zones generous enough for buses and delivery vehicles, while keeping car movements controlled and
away from pedestrian areas
Highways and junctions — confirming every permitted vehicle movement is achievable without encroaching on pedestrian or cyclist space
Our service
Sightline uses industry-leading vehicle tracking software to simulate forward and reverse movements for any vehicle type your project needs —
from passenger cars and buses to articulated trucks and specialised plant. Rather than estimating whether a design will work, we demonstrate it
visually and precisely, giving you and any reviewing authority confidence before construction begins.
We also handle abnormal load assessments for oversized or overweight vehicle movements — wind turbine components, large construction or
modular building elements, and similar loads outside standard vehicle libraries — with custom vehicle modelling and a route-specific review of every
junction, overhead obstacle and sightline involved.
Getting swept path analysis right at the design stage is far more cost-effective than fixing it after construction.

### FAQs
What is swept path analysis?
Testing and confirming the space a vehicle needs to complete a turning manoeuvre, accounting for the fact that a vehicle’s rear wheels track a tighter path
than its front wheels.
Why does my project need it?
If your site needs to accommodate large vehicles — trucks, buses, service or emergency vehicles — this confirms the design will actually work for them
before construction locks it in.
What if my project involves an oversized or unusual vehicle?
We handle abnormal load assessments for vehicles outside standard design libraries, with a route-specific review of every junction and obstacle involved.
Can this be done after construction if there’s an issue?
It can, but it’s far more costly to fix a design flaw after the fact — testing at the design stage is the cost-effective approach.


## Page 07 — Site Audits
**URL:** /site-audits
A traffic management plan is only as effective as its on-site implementation.
Sightline conducts site audits of temporary traffic management set-ups — checking physical implementation against the approved TGS or TMP —
across Sightline-delivered projects, live jobs in progress, and as an independent external auditor for other companies’ plans.
Scope of audit
Compliance against the approved plan — signage, devices, barriers, cones, markings
Signage placement and condition against AS 1742.2 and AS 1742.3
Barrier and deflection zone compliance
Pedestrian and cyclist provisions
Traffic controller positioning and practice
Site access and egress
General housekeeping and hazards affecting the traffic arrangement
Plans are audited regardless of who designed or installed them, providing an independent compliance check for principal contractors, developers,
and councils. Because site conditions change during works, Sightline also audits active, in-progress jobs — including spot audits, re-audits following
staging transitions, and periodic audits across longer-duration projects.
Audits are conducted to the full scope of Transport for NSW requirements, assessed against TCAWS, the relevant Australian Standards, and the
specific approved plan for the site.
Process
1. Review the approved plan governing the site, regardless of who prepared it
2. Conduct an on-site inspection comparing the as-built arrangement against the approved plan
3. Document findings with photographic evidence and location references
4. Compile an audit report detailing compliance, non-compliances, severity, and corrective action
5. Support re-audit following corrective action or a staging transition, to confirm close-out

### FAQs
Can a plan designed and installed by another company be audited?
Yes — reviewed regardless of who prepared it, with findings reported objectively as an independent third party.
Can a job already underway be audited?
Yes — live job audits are core to this service, as compliance can drift during works due to staging changes, damaged devices, or altered conditions.
What is the difference between a site audit and a Road Safety Audit (RSA)?
A site audit checks the set-up against the approved TGS or TMP. An RSA is a broader, independent safety review against general road safety principles,
requiring an Austroads-accredited auditor.
What happens if non-compliances are found on a live job?
Urgent safety issues are flagged for immediate action. Lower-severity items are documented with a recommended correction timeframe.

## Page 09 — Traffic Modelling
**URL:** /traffic-modelling
Test the pressure on your road network before it becomes a real-world problem.
As cities, suburbs, and towns grow, the surrounding road network comes under pressure — from new development, population growth, or changes
to intersection control. Sightline uses mathematical, computer-based models to replicate how a road network operates today and how it will
perform under future conditions.
Process
Existing conditions data — public transport timetables, queue lengths, road geometry, signal phasing, traffic volumes, and travel times
Calibration — the model is checked against real-world observations to confirm it reflects existing conditions
Future scenario testing — a “future base” case is compared against a “future development” case to isolate the impact of a proposed
development
Refinement — the calibrated model tests design changes and mitigation measures ahead of final design
Tools
Analytical models (SIDRA Intersection) — individual intersections or smaller networks requiring a fast, detailed result
Simulation models (Aimsun) — larger, more complex networks, or where an animated, network-wide output is required
Modelling is carried out to the relevant road authority’s guidelines for the project’s jurisdiction (TfNSW, VicRoads, Transport Canberra, and
equivalent bodies elsewhere).

### FAQs
What’s the difference between traffic modelling and a Traffic Impact Assessment?
Traffic modelling is the technical tool. A TIA is the broader report that uses the model’s outputs to assess and justify a development’s traffic impact for a
council or road authority.
Does every development require traffic modelling?
No — generally required where significant traffic demand is expected, particularly in areas already experiencing congestion, or where a road authority
specifically requests it.
What information is required to begin?
Existing conditions data is typically collected as part of the process. Prior traffic studies or site plans, if available, help expedite the work.
Can multiple design options be tested?
Yes — once calibrated, the model tests multiple scenarios to compare options before a final design is committed.


## Page 10 — SIDRA Intersection Capacity Analysis
**URL:** /sidra-intersection-analysis
The data-driven case that convinces council your intersection will actually perform.
SIDRA Intersection is the industry-standard analytical software for detailed, lane-by-lane modelling of traffic flow at intersections, roundabouts, and
signalised networks — mandated by most Australian road authorities, including TfNSW, for development applications affecting intersection
performance.
What SIDRA measures
| Metric | What it means / benchmark |
|---|---|
| Level of Service (LoS) | An A–F grade for how well an intersection is operating — A is free-flowing, F is gridlock. Benchmark: LoS D or better in peak periods. |
| Degree of Saturation (v/c ratio) | How close the intersection is to its capacity limit. Benchmark: below 0.90. |
| Average Delay | Average time a vehicle is stopped or slowed at the intersection. Assessed against LoS. |
| 95th Percentile Queue | The practical maximum queue length, in metres, not exceeded 95% of the time. Must not block driveways or upstream intersections. |
Process
1. Data collection — traffic counts across weekday AM and PM peaks, site geometry, and signal phasing from the relevant authority
2. Model build & calibration — calibrated against observed on-site conditions
3. Scenario testing — a “do nothing” case compared against a “do something” case across opening year and future design horizon

### FAQs
Is SIDRA analysis required for every development application?
No — it depends on project scale and anticipated traffic generation, as determined by the relevant council or road authority.
How does SIDRA differ from simulation software?
SIDRA suits detailed, lane-by-lane analysis of individual intersections. Simulation platforms model vehicle-by-vehicle behaviour across larger networks —
better suited to queue spillback or multi-intersection interactions.
Does SIDRA account for pedestrians and cyclists?
Yes — delay, Level of Service, and crossing time outputs are produced for pedestrians and cyclists as well as vehicles.
What happens if the analysis shows LoS E or F?
It indicates mitigation is required — signal phasing changes, lane reconfiguration, or a physical upgrade — which are modelled to demonstrate a credible
path to an acceptable Level of Service.


## Page 11 *(source note: moved here from the Parking division — copy unchanged)* — Traffic Impact Assessment (TIA)
**URL:** /traffic-impact-assessment
Bigger project, bigger scrutiny.
A Traffic Impact Assessment is the detailed evaluation council or a road authority requires for larger, more complex developments — a full technical
case that your project’s impact on the existing and future transport network has been properly tested, not assumed. Depending on the state, you
might see this called a TIA, TIAR, or TER — the name changes, the underlying requirement doesn’t.
What we assess
Traffic generation & distribution — volume generated and how it disperses through the network
Vehicle access & egress — design and safety of entry and exit points, including ramps
Car parking provision & design — adequacy of on-site parking and AS 2890 / local planning compliance
Safety — conflict points, visibility, pedestrian risk
Sustainable transport — public transport access, bicycle parking, end-of-trip facilities
Waste & loading — safe, efficient collection and delivery operations
When you’ll need one
Typically triggered where a development will generate significant additional traffic volume, alter access arrangements or create new intersections,
affect on-street or off-street parking availability, or impact the safety or efficiency of the surrounding network. Common examples: large shopping
centres, major residential subdivisions, hospitals, large schools, and any project requiring new signals or road upgrades.

### FAQs
How is this different from a TIS?
Depth and scale — full network analysis, intersection modelling such as SIDRA, traffic surveys, and future growth scenarios, versus a shorter statement of
effect.
Does council always call it a “TIA”?
No — naming varies by state, but the content required is consistent.
What’s in the final report?
Existing conditions, the development proposal, traffic generation and distribution analysis (modelled where relevant), parking and access evaluation,
sustainable transport considerations, safety analysis, and final recommendations.

## Page 14 — Traffic Impact Statement (TIS)
**URL:** /traffic-impact-statement
Stalled DA because council wants a traffic report? A Traffic Impact Statement is usually the fix.
It’s a concise technical report demonstrating that your development’s traffic generation, parking, and access arrangements won’t create unsafe or
inefficient conditions on the surrounding road network — the standard requirement for smaller-scale developments where a full assessment would
be overkill.
When a TIS is typically the right call
Small residential flat buildings, commonly under 20 units
Childcare centres
Medical or dental clinics
Small-scale commercial or industrial units
A common rule of thumb is a development expected to generate under 100 vehicle trips in the weekday peak hour — though this is indicative only;
your council or road authority determines the actual requirement.
What’s inside the report
Existing conditions & site analysis — your development’s location, the surrounding road network, intersections, road classification, existing
traffic controls, and nearby public transport, footpath and cycleway access
Traffic generation & parking assessment — expected peak-hour vehicle trips using council or TfNSW rates, a car parking demand check, and
confirmation the layout complies with AS 2890.1
Impact analysis & recommendations — effect on local street performance and safety, access point review, driver sightlines, internal circulation,
and mitigation where needed
A TIS must be prepared by a qualified, appropriately registered traffic engineer — an unqualified preparer is one of the most common, and entirely
avoidable, reasons a report gets rejected outright.

### FAQs
What’s the difference between a TIS and a full TIA?
Scale. A TIS suits smaller developments with localised impact; a TIA is the in-depth study for large-scale projects with network-wide impact, including
intersection modelling.
Do I need a TIS for a simple change of use or small extension?
Possibly — a change of use, say retail to restaurant, carries different traffic and parking characteristics council must assess, even without new building
works. Worth checking before assuming it’s not required.
What happens if the TIS identifies a problem, like a parking shortfall?
We propose a viable fix — revised access design, a parking management plan, or other mitigation — so the concern is resolved with engineering
justification, not left open.
What do you need from me to get started?
A full set of architectural plans (site, floor, elevations), the site address, and a clear project description — dwellings, GFA, or patron and employee numbers,
depending on land use.


## Page 15 — Carpark Demand Assessment
**URL:** /carpark-demand-assessment
Statutory parking rates don’t know your site — a demand assessment does.
Council parking rates are broad, category-based defaults. They don’t account for a site’s proximity to public transport, shared-parking opportunities
in a mixed-use building, or genuine local demand. A Carpark Demand Assessment builds the site-specific, evidence-based case for the parking
provision your project actually needs — whether that’s meeting the standard rate or justifying a reduction.
How we build the case
1. Statutory rates — the default position, applied directly where your land use is a clean fit for the local scheme
2. Empirical research rates — used where your use isn’t listed in the statutory table, drawing on accepted industry data for comparable uses
3. First-principles surveys — for unique or contentious developments with no standard precedent, using new site-specific surveys of comparable
existing developments
What can shift demand below the statutory rate
Proximity to public transport — train stations, high-frequency bus or tram routes
Shared parking in mixed-use buildings where peak periods differ, such as office versus restaurant
Land-use-specific metrics — bedrooms per dwelling, GFA, practitioners or rooms, licensed capacity, seating and attendance
What council receives
A structured report — introduction, methodology referencing the applicable planning clause and rates, a transparent calculation, and a clear
conclusion — backed by tables and architectural plans proving AS 2890.1 compliance.

### FAQs
When should I get this done?
Proactively, before council asks — it’s faster than waiting for a Request for Information and then scrambling to respond.
What if my proposal doesn’t meet the statutory rate?

Generally no — it’s a shared public asset. A survey can show overflow won’t harm local amenity, but your on-site provision still needs to carry the case.

## Page 16 *(source note: Parking Certificate offering folded in here)* — Parking & AS 2890 Compliance
**URL:** /as2890-parking-compliance
One miscalculated dimension can sink a DA.
AS 2890 is the Australian Standard series governing parking facility geometry, and it’s built directly into council DCPs and planning schemes. Non-
compliance is one of the most common — and most preventable — reasons a car park design gets rejected. We check and design against the full
series so it doesn’t happen to your project, and provide the formal parking certification most councils require.
The relevant parts
AS 2890.1 — off-street parking for light vehicles, the standard most commonly applied
AS 2890.2 — off-street commercial vehicle facilities, covering loading docks and heavy vehicle circulation
AS 2890.6 — accessible parking, covering wider bays, adjacent shared zones, and minimum clearances
What we check against AS 2890.1
| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Bay dimensions | 2.4m wide × 5.4m long standard; user class can shift this |
| Headroom | 2.2m standard; 2.5m for accessible spaces and shared areas |
| Aisle width (90°, one-way) | Minimum 6.2m |
| Ramp grade | Max 1 in 4 (25%); transitions required beyond 1 in 8 (12.5%), minimum 2m long |
| Shy distance | Bays next to columns or walls often need widening to 2.7m |
| Wheel stops | 90–100mm high, positioned roughly 0.9m from the front of the space |
| Design vehicle | B85 default; B99 mandated by some councils — confirm early |
Beyond the minimums
Sightlines at driveway exits, lighting, signage and line marking for predictable circulation, and correct treatment of EV charging bays — still subject
to all standard dimensional requirements — all factor into a car park that actually works, not just one that ticks the geometry box.

### FAQs
Is AS 2890.1 legally mandatory?
It’s a Standard, not legislation — but it’s referenced by the NCC and most council DCPs, making compliance a practical requirement for DA approval
regardless.
What’s the difference between AS 2890.1 and AS 2890.6?
2890.1 covers general parking; 2890.6 is the accessible parking standard — wider bays, shared zones, specific signage. They apply together.
Does it matter whether B85 or B99 is used?
Yes — some councils mandate B99 for all or part of a site. Confirming which applies before design avoids a late-stage rejection.
Do I need formal certification, or just a compliant design?
Most councils require formal assessment and certification from a qualified traffic engineer — not just a compliant-looking plan. We issue that certification
as part of this service.


## Page 17 — Driveway Design
**URL:** /driveway-design
A steep site doesn’t have to mean a rejected DA.
Driveway design confirms your entry and exit points are safe and compliant for every vehicle expected to use them — and gradient is where most
sloped-site designs fail, usually because the transitions weren’t calculated properly.
Core gradient rules (AS 2890.1)
Maximum grade within the property: 1 in 4 (25%)
Maximum grade at the property line or footpath crossing: 1 in 20 (5%), extending 6m into the site to protect sightlines
Transitions required wherever a grade change exceeds 1 in 8 (12.5%) — minimum 2m long, at roughly half the difference between the two grades,
preventing underside scraping at summits and sags
Design vehicles modelled at 120mm ground clearance to verify the full swept path stays clear
Engineering for steep sites
Switchbacks — horizontal curves that extend travel distance to bring the effective grade back under 1 in 4
Flat landing zones — required top and bottom, maximum 1 in 20, giving vehicles a safe pause point before entering traffic
Swept path verification — confirms longer vehicles clear the inner kerb and stay off opposing lanes on curved layouts
Surface selection — solid, textured surfaces on steeper grades for wet-weather traction; loose gravel isn’t suitable
What council wants to see
Longitudinal sections showing existing versus proposed levels, cross-sections at the property boundary and garage entry, and formal certification
confirming ground clearance holds throughout.

### FAQs
What’s the steepest a driveway can be?
1 in 4 (25%) within the property, dropping to 1 in 20 (5%) for the first 6m at the street.
How do you stop vehicles scraping on a steep driveway?
Transition grades — at least 2m long, roughly half the main slope — at every point where the grade change exceeds 1 in 8.
Does this apply to single residential driveways too?
Yes — AS 2890.1 governs all off-street driveway design, including houses on steep or constrained sites.
What if full compliance genuinely isn’t achievable on my site?
Councils can accept a “Performance Solution” — a more detailed engineering case proving an alternative design is still safe and functional.


## Page 18 — Pedestrian Sight Line Assessment
**URL:** /sight-line-assessment
The most overlooked reason a DA gets knocked back.
A sight line assessment confirms a driver exiting your property can actually see pedestrians and oncoming traffic before crossing the footpath —
and vice versa. It’s a core AS 2890.1 safety requirement, not paperwork, and a frequent point of rejection when it’s skipped.
The sight triangle
AS 2890.1 Figure 3.3 defines the area that must stay clear using two measurements:
‘X’ distance — driver eye setback from the property line, typically a minimum of 2.5m for standard vehicles
‘Y’ distance — required line of sight along the footpath, commonly 2.0m in each direction for low-volume driveways
Anything taller than 1.0m inside this triangle counts as an obstruction.
What commonly gets in the way
Solid walls and fencing, dense landscaping, structural or utility columns, and street furniture — letterboxes, signage, even parked cars — are the
usual culprits. Higher pedestrian activity or faster adjacent traffic both push the required distances up further.
How we assess it
1. Desktop review of architectural and landscape plans, flagging likely obstructions early
2. CAD analysis — a precise digital model plotting the required sight triangles against your actual layout
3. Reporting — a formal compliance statement, with clear recommendations wherever the design falls short

### FAQs
Are mirrors an acceptable fix?
Generally a last resort — councils prefer compliant geometry over a mirror’s failure points such as damage, distortion and maintenance.
Does this cover both entering and exiting?
Yes, though exiting is usually the primary focus.
Do single dwellings need a formal assessment?
Not always requested formally, but the minimum distances still apply — more likely to be formally checked on busier roads or constrained sites.
What happens if my design fails?
We recommend a fix — relocating the driveway, adjusting the layout, or lowering fencing and landscaping — matched to what your site can actually
accommodate.
